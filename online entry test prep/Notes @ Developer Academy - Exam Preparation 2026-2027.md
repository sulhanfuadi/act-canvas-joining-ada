
## SECTION 1 - Learning & Thinking
### 1.1 Critical Thinking

Every day, we're surrounded by arguments, claims, and opinions - from social media posts to news articles to conversations with friends. Critical thinking is the skill of evaluating these claims carefully: asking whether the reasoning is sound, whether the evidence supports the conclusion, and whether there might be flaws in the logic.

Strong critical thinkers don't just accept or reject ideas based on gut feeling, they examine arguments systematically, looking for common reasoning errors called logical fallacies.

SECTION 3 - Computational Thinking

Computational thinking is a way of approaching problems that's valuable far beyond programming. It's about breaking down complex challenges, recognizing patterns, filtering out irrelevant details, and creating systematic solutions.

You don't need to be a programmer to join the Academy - that's what you'll learn there. This section helps you develop the mindset that makes learning to code much easier: following logical steps, tracing processes, predicting outcomes, and spotting where something went wrong.

3.1 What Is Computational Thinking

Despite the name, computational thinking is not about computers, but about solving problems systematically. It involves four key skills: decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, and algorithmic thinking. You already use these skills in everyday life, often without realizing it. This subsection introduces the overall framework before practicing specific techniques.

3.2 Abstraction

Abstraction is the practice of simplifying complexity by focusing only on what matters and hiding unnecessary details. From using appliances to reading maps or following recipes, abstraction helps us manage complexity and make problems more approachable.

Resources

3.3 Decomposition

Decomposition means breaking large, complex problems into smaller, manageable pieces. Each piece is easier to understand and solve, and together they address the original challenge. This approach applies to everything from planning events to writing essays to building software.

Resources

3.4 Pattern Recognition

Pattern recognition is the ability to identify similarities, regularities, and recurring structures. Recognizing patterns allows us to reuse solutions, make predictions, and form general rules. This skill is fundamental to computational thinking because it helps us apply solutions from one context to another.

3.5 Conditional Logic

Conditional logic describes situations where outcomes depend on conditions: if something is true, then something else happens. This type of reasoning appears everywhere — from everyday decisions to rules, contracts, and the algorithms you'll eventually learn to create.
